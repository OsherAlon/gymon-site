How to estimate your 1RM without maxing out

Drafted with AI assistance from the sources listed below · Not yet reviewed by the editor · Published Oct 7, 2026 · 2 min read

Bottom line

One hard set of a few reps can replace a max test, if you read the result as an estimate.

The idea

A normal hard set already holds the answer

Testing a true one-rep max takes a long warm-up, a spotter and a fair bit of nerve. A formula gets you close from a set you were going to do anyway[]. You put in the weight and the reps, and an estimated max comes out the other end.

The catch

The same formula doesn't fit everyone

Research suggests that people who are used to long sets squeeze out more reps at the same load[]. In one small study, endurance runners did about twice as many reps as weightlifters at 70% of their max[]. So if you estimate from a light, long set, the number can flatter you by a fair margin.

Sweet spot

Heavier sets give steadier estimates

The gap between the two groups closed as the weight got heavier[]. That makes a heavy set of a few reps a lot more trustworthy than a long set of fifteen. Pick a weight that feels genuinely heavy, but one you can still move with clean form.

Which formula

Epley and Brzycki tell a similar story

Both formulas turn your reps into an estimated max, and the calculator below shows them side by side. Epley's version began as a coaching chart rather than a study[]. That is one more reason to read the result as a rough guide, not a precise figure.

What it is for

It works best as a trend line

A single estimate on its own says very little, because a good or bad day can move it. The same lift, done the same way week after week, shows whether your strength is moving. That holds even when no single number is exactly right.

How to apply it

  1. 1

    Step 1: Pick a lift you know well

    Choose one you already do every week with steady form, like a squat, bench press or row.

  2. 2

    Step 2: Warm up, then do one hard set

    Use a weight you can lift only a few times, and stop before your form starts to break.

  3. 3

    Step 3: Enter the weight and reps

    Put them into the calculator below and write down the estimate it gives you, with the date.

  4. 4

    Step 4: Keep the set the same

    Same lift, same depth, same rest before it, so next time is a fair comparison.

  5. 5

    Step 5: Compare over weeks

    A rising estimate means you are getting stronger, even in weeks when the bar barely moves.

  6. 6

    Step 6: Retest every few weeks

    Use the estimate as a check-in, not a workout goal, and keep most of your sets well short of failure.

Sources

Study details, sizes and how certain each finding is.

  1. Richens B, Cleather DJ (2014). The relationship between the number of repetitions performed at given intensities is different in endurance and strength trained athletes. Biology of Sport 31(2):157–161. DOI 10.5604/20831862.1099047. PMID 24899782.

    What it found: Eight weightlifters and eight endurance runners lifted to failure at 70%, 80% and 90% of their leg-press max. Runners did far more reps at 70% (39.9 vs 17.9) and at 80%, but not at 90%.

“Eight weightlifters and eight endurance runners each completed a one repetition maximum test on the leg press and completed repetitions to fatigue at 90, 80 and 70% of their one repetition maximum. The endurance runners completed significantly more repetitions than the weightlifters at 70% (39.9 ± 17.6 versus 17.9 ± 2.8; p < 0.05) and 80% (19.8 ± 6.4 versus 11.8 ± 2.7; p < 0.05) of their one repetition maximum but not at 90% (10.8 ± 3.9 versus 7.0 ± 2.1; p > 0.05) of one repetition maximum. … Epley [ 8 ] A poundage chart, not based on scientific research.”
